## Getting Here

I began dancing again in 2016 after a long break. When returning I found an amazing blues jam in SLC that happens every monday night at The Green Pig (probably still going). Every monday, about 10-15 musicians show up to jam slow tempo blues songs and about 10-15 dancers show to dance. This is among the random collection of people out for a drink. I was hooked to going every week and learning by doing. 

Then came a lindy hop exchange hosted in SLC. Many of my new friends were going so it made sense to go too. This became a pivotal event that changed the course of my life. Many of the concepts I learned there I still practice and think about today. It started me going deeper into lindy hop, adjacent swing dances, and on to all sorts of other styles.

Now more than a decade later, I'm running a fusion dance scene in Eugene, Oregon; traveling to teach and DJ at weekend events; and immersed contining to learning all different styles of partner dancing.

## Blues vs Fusion

Shortly after starting blues dancing in 2016, I began attending blues weekends as well. They were incredible! I loved the grove and playfulness. I went to so many different blues idiomatic dance classes and just learned the style. This was also when "fusion" rooms were gaining popularity at all the events. I further found myself drawn to the sensual and intimate form dance in these rooms.

Then came an awareness expansion of racism and systemic privilege of white people. I was not directly involved but heard the rumblings of the arguments and have felt the impact.

**Full disclosure:** I am a cis-gendered white male. I have been raised in a culture that systematically socialized me to have particular biases and blind spots. I will make mistakes and get things wrong here. I value feedback and opportunity to grow.

Racism. While not directly involved in the arguments, they went something like this: blues is an dance style that originates from lindy hop and black culture. Much of the blues scene when I started was white, run by white organizers, and taught by white instructors. These people, while well intentioned and loving the dance style, were benefiting financially from something black people created. Furthermore, as "fusion" began changing the music and style it was beginning to look less like "blues". Layer on different forms of racism, white guilt, and shame and there was a recipe for conflict. 

I won't catalog the participants and specifics here. At a high level though there were primary groups organized around one dance style that was danced to "blues" music artists like nina simon and "fusion" music by artists lik Marian Hill. Pressure around racism largely caused white organizers to drop "blues" and lean into the "fusion" events. This has allowed many to side step the argument of racism because it's no longer "blues". While there's been some progress in learning more and providing more opportunties to BIPOC teachers, organizers, and instructors, fusion scenes remain largely white and organized by white people (myself included).

For the rest of this article I'm going to define:

* "Blues": grounded, improvised African American partner dancing to blues music (e.g. Muddy Waters “Hoochie Coochie Man,” Etta James “I’d Rather Go Blind,” Bessie Smith “St. Louis Blues.”)
* "Fusion": the newer improvised partner dancing that blends any style’s technique to fit the music. (e.g. Massive Attack “Teardrop,” Bonobo “Kerala,” Gotan Project “Santa María,” Emancipator.)

## The "Death" of Blues

One impact of this I felt acutely is the dramatic decline in blues dances (see "blues" definition above). This was driving largely by the blues and fusion divide, also largely by covid in 2020. Coming back from Covid, there were almost no blues weekend events while fusion was still very prevelant.
